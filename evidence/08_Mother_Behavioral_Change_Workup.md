## 1. SAFETY FIRST

You are describing escalating violence in a shared household where you are also the caregiver. That combination has three risks and they are all real: injury to you, injury to her, and an allegation against you.

**If there is immediate danger:** 911. Say the address, that an adult with a psychiatric condition is violent, and whether there is a weapon.

**If it is a mental-health crisis without immediate danger:** call or text **988**.

**Family violence resources that apply to caregiving situations:** National Domestic Violence Hotline, **1-800-799-7233**, or text START to **88788**. Free, 24 hours, and they handle adult-child-to-parent and caregiver situations. **211** routes to Orange County behavioral health crisis services.

**Three rules when a situation is escalating:**
1. Leave the room before the argument becomes physical. Do not block a doorway, do not take an object from her hands, do not raise your voice to match hers.
2. Call for help *while* it is happening, not after. A same-day call creates a record; a call three days later does not.
3. Photograph any injury the same day, with a date-stamped image, and write down what happened within an hour. Memory degrades; a note does not.

**California recording law:** all-party consent for confidential communications, Penal Code § 632. Do not secretly record her. Write it down instead. A contemporaneous written log is admissible; a secret recording is a problem.

## 3. THE HONEST MEDICAL PICTURE

You raised lead. It is a reasonable question and it is testable. But if you walk into a clinic and say "lead caused this," you will be dismissed, and the visit will be wasted. Here is what is actually true, and what to ask for instead.

### 3.1 What the site data actually shows for lead
Soil lead was **84.2 mg/kg, with a maximum of 145 mg/kg**. The California residential screening level for lead is **80 mg/kg** (DTSC, 2019 — confirm with counsel or the testing lab). So the lead is **modestly above** the residential screening level, not at smelter-site concentrations. That matters: it is enough to justify a blood test, and it is not enough to support a claim that lead alone caused a psychiatric change. If a report overstates it, the report becomes attackable.

### 3.2 What is more neurotoxic on that site than the lead
This is the part almost nobody gets right:
- **Arsenic** (9.36 mg/kg) — neurotoxic, and it crosses into the CNS.
- **Mercury** — neurotoxic, and vapor from buried debris is an inhalation pathway.
- **TCE and PCE** in soil gas — chlorinated solvents with well-documented neurological and psychiatric effects, including irritability, mood change, and cognitive slowing. **These vaporize. They enter through the floor.**
- **Toluene, benzene** — CNS depressants.

If the hypothesis is "something under that floor is affecting her brain," **TCE and PCE in soil gas are the stronger candidate than lead**, and they are also the pathway the November 7, 2023 photographs document: a breached cap, in a structure with a failed base seal.

### 3.3 The four explanations you must not skip
A good physician will test all of these, and so should you, because if one of them is the answer, that is better for her than a five-year mystery.

1. **Post-intensive care syndrome (PICS).** She had septic shock, intubation, and a 36-day admission followed by 26 days in a skilled nursing facility. Cognitive impairment, depression, anxiety, and irritability after critical illness are common and well documented. This is a real, diagnosable, treatable condition — and it fits her timeline precisely.
2. **Delirium and post-sepsis cognitive change.** Sepsis survivors have measurable cognitive and psychiatric sequelae for months to years.
3. **Medication effects and interactions.** Her regimen has included vancomycin, ceftriaxone, metronidazole, azithromycin, fluconazole, acyclovir, linezolid, doxycycline — and linezolid in particular has known neuropsychiatric effects. Plus whatever she takes now. **Bring the full pharmacy printout, not a list from memory.**
4. **Untreated or undertreated primary psychiatric illness.** You said she was ill before 2021. That is the baseline. An exposure can worsen a condition without being its origin, and both can be true at once.

### 3.4 What you can actually measure
| Test | What it shows | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood lead level (BLL)** | Recent exposure (half-life ~30 days). Cheap, fast, covered by Medi-Cal | Any lab; ask by name |
| **Urine arsenic, mercury** (speciated) | Current body burden of the neurotoxic metals on that site | Lab, with physician order |
| **CMP, CBC, TSH, B12, folate** | Reversible causes of confusion and irritability | Standard panel |
| **Neuropsychological testing** | Objective baseline of memory, attention, executive function | Referral; often covered |
| **Psychiatric evaluation** | Diagnosis, medication review, safety assessment | County behavioral health or Medi-Cal plan |

**Ask the physician this exact question:** *"She is a former resident of a capped brownfield shelter with documented Cr(VI), arsenic, mercury, chlorinated solvents in soil gas, and lead above the residential screening level. She has a documented behavioral change since 2023. What workup would you order, and can we start with a blood lead level and a urine metals panel?"*

That question is answerable. "Did lead cause this?" is not.

### 3.5 One test that is not standard
Bone lead measurement (K-XRF) reflects cumulative exposure over decades, which is what you would want for exposure that ended in 2024. It is largely a research tool, not a clinical order. Do not spend money on it before a physician says it is worth it.

## 4. HOW TO BUILD THE BEHAVIORAL RECORD — THE A-B-C LOG

This is the part that converts five years of experience into evidence. One entry per incident, written within an hour, in a bound notebook or a single file. **No adjectives. No diagnosis. No motive.**

| Field | Example of a correct entry |
|---|---|
| Date and time | 03/14/2026, 8:40 p.m. |
| Place | [address or shelter] |
| What happened immediately before | "Asked her to take her evening medication." |
| What she did | "Threw a glass against the wall. Glass broke. Struck my forearm." |
| What she said | Quote it exactly, in quotation marks, without commentary. |
| Injury | "Two-inch scratch, left forearm. Photographed 8:52 p.m." |
| Who else saw it | Name and role |
| What I did | "Left the room. Called 911 at 8:47 p.m. Report #____." |
| Aftermath | "Slept in the other room. No further incidents." |

**Why this format:** a log with dates, quotes, injuries, and report numbers is evidence. A narrative that says "she has become violent" is an opinion, and opinions about a person's mental state are the easiest thing in the world to attack.

**What the log will show a doctor:** onset, frequency, severity, triggers, and trajectory — which is exactly what distinguishes a psychiatric progression from an exposure effect from a medication reaction.

**What the log will show a lawyer:** that the change is real, dated, and witnessed, and that it began after a specific period of residence. That is the difference between a claim and a story.
