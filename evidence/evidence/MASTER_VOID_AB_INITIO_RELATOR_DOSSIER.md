## 2.1 The Statutory Mandate: Judicial Notice Is Non-Discretionary Under FRE 201(c)(2)
Federal Rule of Evidence 201 governs judicial notice of adjudicative facts. Rule 201(b) establishes:
"The court may judicially notice a fact that is not subject to reasonable dispute because it:
(1) is generally known within the trial court's territorial jurisdiction; or
(2) can be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned."

While Rule 201(c)(1) permits discretionary notice, Rule 201(c)(2) contains a non-waivable statutory command:
"The court... MUST take judicial notice if a party requests it and the court is supplied with the necessary information."

Under Rule 201(d), judicial notice may be taken at any stage of the proceeding—including on a motion to dismiss, motion for default judgment, summary judgment, or sua sponte by the court.

## 2.2 Controlling Ninth Circuit Precedent Authorizes and Requires Notice of Intra-Court and Inter-Tribunal Records
The United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit has repeatedly and unequivocally held that district courts must take judicial notice of court filings, official dockets, admissions, and proceedings in other cases pending before the same court or district:

- **United States v. Wilson, 631 F.2d 118, 119 (9th Cir. 1980):**
  "It is well established that a court may take judicial notice of its own records in other cases, as well as the records of an inferior court in other cases."
- **Headwaters Inc. v. U.S. Forest Service, 399 F.3d 1047, 1051 n.3 (9th Cir. 2005):**
  The Ninth Circuit held unequivocally: "Materials from a proceeding in another tribunal are appropriate for judicial notice."
- **Reyn's Pasta Bella LLC v. Visa USA, Inc., 442 F.3d 741, 746 n.6 (9th Cir. 2006):**
  The Ninth Circuit affirmed that courts "may take judicial notice of court filings and other matters of public record," specifically holding that briefs, settlement papers, dockets, and hearing transcripts in prior or related litigation are appropriate subjects of judicial notice.
- **Harris v. County of Orange, 682 F.3d 1126, 1132 (9th Cir. 2012):**
  The Ninth Circuit reversed a district court for failing to notice county records and court dockets, reaffirming that federal courts in the Central District of California may judicially notice official county documents, municipal board minutes, and public court dockets.
- **United States v. Black, 482 F.3d 1035, 1041 (9th Cir. 2007):**
  Courts may take judicial notice of proceedings and filings in other courts, both within and without the federal judicial system, if those proceedings have a direct relation to matters at issue.
- **Castillo-Villagra v. INS, 972 F.2d 1017, 1026 (9th Cir. 1992):**
  Official administrative records and legislative resolutions are appropriate subjects of mandatory judicial notice.
